First layer: Mitra/Mithra as covenant. The Indo-Iranian name is tied to a word meaning contract, covenant, agreement, treaty, alliance, or promise. So the oldest center is not “sun god” in the simple sense. It is binding relation: the thing sworn between parties, and the power that watches whether it is kept. 
Second layer: Mithra as watcher and judge. In the Mihr Yasht, Mithra is imagined with watchposts, spies, broad vision, and power against contract-breakers. He protects paths, sees wrongdoing, judges living beings, and catches the liar wherever they try to hide. 
Third layer: Roman Mithras. In the Roman mystery cult, Mithras becomes a cave/cosmos figure: initiation, planets, zodiac, torchbearers, bull-slaying, and cosmic order. The famous bull-slaying image is not just “man kills bull”; it is a whole symbolic diagram. But scholars are careful: Roman Mithraism is related to Iranian Mithra, yet not simply the same religion copied westward. The continuity question is debated. 
The clean symbolic reading is this: Mithra is the god of the witnessed bond. Not the throne. Not the crown. Not arbitrary force. He is the force that says, “A promise was made; now show the record.” Light matters because the oath must be visible. Fire matters because ordeal and truth-testing attach to the oath.
